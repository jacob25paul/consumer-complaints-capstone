## Speaker notes

### 1. Routing consumer complaints and finding the ones that end in monetary relief (0.75 min)

Hi, thanks for making time. In the next twenty minutes I'll walk through my third capstone: two models that help a complaint-handling team at a bank or credit bureau. The first routes each complaint to the right product team. The second ranks complaints by how likely they are to end with the company paying money back, so the review team can look at those first. I'll cover the problem, the data and its limits, the key wrangling and EDA findings, how I set up a leakage-free time-based evaluation, the models, the test results, and what I'd recommend. I'll keep the last ten minutes for your questions.

### 2. Complaint teams do two jobs by hand: route each complaint, and decide which to review first (1.25 min)

Banks, lenders and credit bureaus receive large volumes of complaints through the CFPB. Two things happen to every one of them. First, someone reads it and routes it to the team that handles that product: mortgages, cards, credit reports and so on. Second, a compliance team decides which complaints to look at first, ideally the ones involving real financial harm. My proposal turned these into two supervised tasks. Routing: predict the product, judged by macro-F1 so that small products count as much as big ones. Relief: rank complaints by the chance the company closes them with monetary relief rather than just an explanation, judged by average precision and precision in the top ten percent, because that is how a review queue would use it. And one question runs through the whole project: does the consumer's free-text narrative add anything beyond the fields of the complaint form?

### 3. 17.5 million complaints, but narratives exist for only about one in five, and almost none after 2025 (1.25 min)

The data is the CFPB's narratives archive: 21 exports with 17,533,918 complaints from 2011 to August 2026. It is too big for my laptop, so I used a uniform random 20 percent sample, which keeps every rate unbiased. Two limits shaped everything. First, narratives are opt-in: about 22 percent of complaints have one, they only start in 2015, and the right panel shows opt-in collapsing in January 2026 before the CFPB stopped publishing narratives in August. Second, outcome maturity: the left panel shows the share of complaints still in progress. It is near zero until mid-2026, then 16 percent in July and 56 percent in August. Training on those months would teach the model that recent complaints never get relief, so I excluded them, and I treat the rest of 2026 as a separate out-of-distribution check. That leaves 758,548 usable narratives.

### 4. Four wrangling decisions, each forced by what the data showed (1.25 min)

Four wrangling decisions mattered most, and each came from checking the data rather than assuming. Duplicates: the obvious rule, same text, company, product, issue and date, flagged 61,142 narratives, but most of those groups came from different ZIP codes. They are template letters sent by different people, not re-submissions, so I dropped only the 3,726 true same-consumer repeats and flagged the templates. Products: the CFPB renamed and split its product list over the years, so I mapped 21 raw labels onto today's 11 products, using the sub-product to resolve labels that were later split. Relief: I mapped each of the eight response labels explicitly and followed the proposal's target. And I flagged outliers instead of deleting them, and kept missing values as explicit categories, because the missingness is informative.

### 5. Relief depends mostly on the product and the company (1.25 min)

The first EDA finding is the most important for modelling. Whether a complaint ends in monetary relief depends mostly on what it is about and who it is against. Among narrative complaints, prepaid cards get relief almost 20 percent of the time and credit reporting 0.1 percent. By company, large banks and card issuers are far above the credit bureaus and debt buyers, which are near zero. The categorical association tests agree: issue, product and company have the strongest association with relief, a Cramér's V of about a third, while state and ZIP are weak. The implication: a model that only uses the form fields will already rank complaints well, so the bar for showing that the narrative adds value is high. These bars use notebook 1's exploratory relief definition; the modelling uses the proposal's definition.

### 6. Everything drifts over time, so the evaluation has to be time-based (1.25 min)

The second finding is drift. The left panel shows narrative volume growing quickly from 2022. The middle panel shows the product mix shifting toward credit reporting, which becomes about three quarters of narratives. The right panel shows the relief rate falling from 7.8 percent in 2015 to 1.3 percent in 2025. Much of that fall is the mix change, since credit reporting almost never ends in relief. Then 2026 jumps to 6.7 percent, but that is a composition effect: credit reporting drops to 4 percent of 2026 narratives. Two consequences. A random train-test split would mix years and overstate performance, so I split by time. And 2026 is a different population, so it is kept out of training and testing and reported separately.

### 7. The narrative carries signal, a quoted dollar amount most of all (1 min)

The third finding is that the text itself carries signal. Complaints whose narrative quotes a dollar amount get relief 7.6 percent of the time, against 1.2 percent when no amount is quoted. That is the largest effect among the text-shape features, with a rank-biserial of 0.45. Relief narratives are also longer. And complaints whose exact text appears in another complaint, the template letters, almost never get relief: 0.01 percent against 3.6 percent for unique narratives. These are all things a TF-IDF model can pick up, so there is reason to expect the narrative to help, and the question becomes how much beyond the form fields.

### 8. Fitted on the past, tested on the future, with no leakage (1.5 min)

This is the training-data design. The split is by complaint date: train on March 2015 to June 2024, validate on the second half of 2024, and test once on all of 2025. Template letters could leak across that boundary, so any validation or test narrative whose exact text appeared earlier is removed. Every transformer is fitted on training rows only, inside scikit-learn pipelines. Categorical fields get one-hot dummies, with rare levels lumped together, so 3,687 companies become 506 columns and new companies in 2025 are handled safely. The skewed count features are log-transformed and standardised for the linear models; I don't scale them for the trees, because tree splits don't depend on scale. Text becomes TF-IDF on words and word pairs. And for leakage: post-outcome fields are never loaded, the date isn't a feature, and for routing I exclude issue and sub-product, which are chosen together with the product.

### 9. Five models per task: text plus form fields wins both (1.25 min)

I compared five models per task, from trivial to strongest, all tuned on the 2024 validation half-year only. For routing, the majority class scores a macro-F1 of 0.079. The form fields alone, mostly the company, reach 0.557. The narrative alone reaches 0.679, and narrative plus form fields is best at 0.733. LightGBM on selected TF-IDF features came close at 0.713 but didn't beat the regularised linear model, which is common with sparse text. For relief, the ranking is LightGBM on text and structured fields at an AP of 0.406, then the text plus structured logistic regression, then the structured-only models. The structured-only LightGBM reaches 0.386, which shows how much the form already explains. I treated class weighting as a setting to tune: balanced weights actually lowered routing macro-F1 in every model.

### 10. Routing on 2025: macro-F1 0.70, against 0.56 with form fields only and 0.08 for the majority class (1.25 min)

Now the test year, 2025, which the model never saw. Routing reaches a macro-F1 of 0.702 and accuracy of 0.90, against 0.558 for the form-fields-only model and 0.076 for always predicting credit reporting. So the narrative clearly helps routing. The left matrix is recall by true product, the right is precision by predicted product. Eight of the eleven products have precision and recall of at least 0.65. The biggest single error is debt collection being routed to credit reporting, 31 percent of debt-collection complaints. Collections appear on credit reports, and when I read the most confident cases, they are entirely credit-report disputes, so part of this is label noise. The weak classes are small and overlapping: debt or credit management, prepaid cards, and payday loans.

### 11. The model's confidence is usable: 76% of complaints could be auto-routed at 96.5% accuracy (0.75 min)

Because a routing team has to trust the model, I checked whether its confidence means anything. The left chart compares the top-class probability with actual accuracy: they track each other closely. That makes a simple policy possible. If we auto-route only complaints where the model is at least 90 percent confident, that covers 76 percent of complaints with 96.5 percent accuracy. The remaining quarter, where accuracy is 69 percent, goes to a person. The right chart shows the whole trade-off, so a team can pick its own threshold.

### 12. Relief on 2025: the top 10% of the review queue holds 86% of all relief cases (1.25 min)

For relief on 2025, the final LightGBM model reaches an average precision of 0.322. The base rate is only 2.2 percent, and the form-fields-only model reaches 0.296. The left chart is the precision-recall curve: the final model, in red, sits above the structured-only one, especially at the high-precision end. The right chart is the one a review team cares about. If they read complaints in score order, the top 10 percent of the queue already contains 85.9 percent of all relief cases, 2,574 of 2,997. That is a lift of 8.6 over reading at random. Precision in the top decile is 0.189, close to its ceiling of 0.22, since there are fewer relief cases than slots in the top decile.

### 13. The narrative's gain is real but small, and largest where the form fields can't help (1.25 min)

Back to the central question: does the narrative add value for relief? A bootstrap over the test set gives an AP gain of 0.026 with a 95 percent interval from 0.013 to 0.038, and the gain is positive in every one of the thousand resamples. So it's real. But it's small in operational terms: top-decile precision only moves by about 0.003. Where does it help? The left chart shows it's at the very top of the queue: 46 percent of the top 1 percent are relief cases, against 43 percent for structured-only. The right chart computes AP within each product, where the product can't help. In credit reporting the narrative lifts AP from 0.047 to 0.178, and similarly in debt collection. These are the products where relief is rare and the form fields can't find it, but the text can.

### 14. What the model gets wrong is mostly not visible in the text (1.25 min)

What does the model get wrong? Three patterns. Mis-routes cluster where products genuinely overlap. The biggest is debt collection going to credit reporting, and the confident cases read like credit-report disputes, so part of that is label noise. Missed relief cases, 423 or 14 percent of them, fall outside the top decile. They are concentrated in low-relief products, they are shorter, and they quote a dollar amount far less often. Typically they are generic credit-report disputes where nothing in the text points to money. False alarms at the top of the queue are mostly fee and unrecognised-charge complaints at banks and card issuers. They look exactly like relief cases but were closed with an explanation, and many probably still deserve a look. Finally, the model partly learns which companies tend to pay. It predicts the company's recorded response, not whether the consumer was harmed.

### 15. Recommendations, limitations and next steps (1.5 min)

Three recommendations. One: automate routing for confident predictions. A 0.9 threshold covers about three quarters of complaints at about 96 percent accuracy, and the rest, plus the three weak classes, go to a triage queue. Two: use the relief score to order the review queue, not to decide outcomes. Reviewing the top 10 percent finds 86 percent of relief cases. Three: recalibrate the probabilities on recent months before showing them as percentages, because they run a bit high, 2.9 percent predicted against 2.2 percent actual. On limitations, I want to be direct. The narrative adds only a little to relief prediction overall. Issue routing is weak at 0.31 macro-F1. The labels are company-recorded, the narratives are opt-in, and the archive is frozen. On the changed 2026 population, routing drops to 0.65 and relief lift falls to 4.7, so the models must be re-validated before use. Next I'd harmonise issue labels, test sentence embeddings, and recalibrate.

### 16. Three takeaways (0.75 min)

To sum up in three points. Routing works and is ready for confident cases: a macro-F1 of 0.70 on an unseen year, with three quarters of complaints routable automatically at 96.5 percent accuracy. Relief ranking works: the top 10 percent of the queue holds 86 percent of relief cases, but most of that comes from the product and the company. And the narrative helps, significantly but modestly, and most where the form fields are uninformative. Everything is in the repo: three notebooks, the report and the metrics file. Thank you, I'm happy to take questions.
